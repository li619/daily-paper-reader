Abstract 

Recovering obscured text in palimpsests, i.e. the manuscripts where the original writing (under-text) was erased and over-written for reuse, is vital for preserving lost information. Due to the nature of the material, such a task can be highly challenging and time-consuming. This pa-per presents an approach towards auto-mated palimpsest handwritten text model-ing for under-text recovery. We investi-gate generative models, including Genera-tive Adversarial Networks (GANs) and dif-fusion models, and design a custom de-noising diffusion model that learns struc-tured noise patterns to reconstruct the under-text. The recovered text is subsequently processed by a fine-tuned, attention-based handwritten text recognition network. Fur-thermore, we investigate a language-guided recognition framework that leverages tex-tual class descriptions as informative pri-ors, enabling the text recognition model to distinguish meaningful features from noise. This is achieved by grounding task-specific descriptions, phrased in words or sen-tences, through visual attention maps gen-erated by pretrained vision-language mod-els like CLIP (Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training). To facilitate future research and benchmarking, we release new datasets for synthetic palimpsests (currently scarce), CLIP-compatible text descriptions, and pre-trained models with code. 

1 Introduction 

Palimpsests are historical manuscripts that contain a visible top layer of text and one or more hid-den, erased layers beneath it. The original writings were scraped-off or overwritten to reuse the writ-ing material (parchment). Restoring palimpsests and recovering the underlying handwriting is fun-damentally an inverse problem, one that aims to reverse the processes of erasure and overwriting to reveal the lost text. While multispectral and hyperspectral imaging can enhance contrast between inks and parchment, other factors such as spectral overlap, surface ir-regularities, and noise often obscure the faint sig-nals of the under-text. Because these limitations constrain the effectiveness of imaging-based tech-niques, they have prompted exploration of data-driven approaches, such as deep learning. How-ever, emerging deep learning approaches face their own limitations, due to the scarcity of annotated training data, poor generalisation across different manuscripts, and the risk of generating misleading reconstructions. The field remains significantly under-explored, and to the best of our knowledge, no existing method can reliably separate over-text from under-text across diverse manuscripts and perform accurate recognition. This gap highlights the need for standardised, reproducible workflows for manuscript restoration. Recent advances in handwritten text recognition (HTR) have leveraged natural language processing (NLP) techniques to improve recognition accuracy (Li et al., 2025; Chen and Ströbel, 2024). Lan-guage models, textual priors, and semantic embed-dings provide contextual information that helps disambiguate visually uncertain predictions, while vision-language frameworks enable grounding of textual descriptions in visual features. Integrating NLP with HTR transforms it into a multimodal problem, allowing models to distinguish relevant text from noise and better handle challenging sce-narios such as palimpsests. Our work builds on this line of research and ex-amines the extent to which natural language guid-ance can improve the reconstruction and recog-nition of under-text in palimpsests using a deep learning framework. Our key contributions: a) We examine how generative models, including Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) and arXiv:2610.24060v1 [cs.CV] 16 Oct 2026 diffusion models, can enhance under-text ex-traction, and introduce a custom denoising dif-fusion model (cDDM) tailored to this problem. b) We introduce a language-guided, attention-based recognition framework that leverages textual priors to guide HTR. c) We explore a new distillation paradigm that transfers knowledge via guided attention and integrates CLIP-based vision-language grounding to align semantic text descriptions with visual features, enabling the model to dis-tinguish handwriting from noise. d) The proposed approach is model-agnostic and broadly transferable to any HTR framework that exposes intermediate activation maps and utilises a standard classification loss. e) To address the scarcity of training data, we cre-ate and release synthetic palimpsest datasets, CLIP-compatible text descriptions, and pre-trained models, providing resources for future research and benchmarking. The source code for our implementation is publicly available at https://github.com/ UU-VLL/GTM-LGHTR-Palimpsest . The dataset is available at https://doi.org/10. 5281/zenodo.21902987 .

2 Related Work 

When it comes to palimpsest restoration, tradi-tional approaches, such as colour space transfor-mation (Tonazzini, 2010), and statistical methods, such as Principal Component Analysis and In-dependent Component Analysis on multi-spectral imaging (Salerno et al., 2007; Easton et al., 2011), have long been the leading techniques for text extraction and layer separation. Recently, deep learning models, including Convolutional Neural Networks (Perino et al., 2023), generative inpaint-ing (Jampour et al., 2024), latent diffusion mod-els (Jampour, 2025), and the increasingly pop-ular GANs (Starynska et al., 2021; Madi et al., 2023), have begun to play a more important role in this field. In particular, Madi et al. (2023) com-pared several GAN models and showed that their proposed models, Palimpsest Manuscripts Recon-struction (PMR)-GAN, and Pix2pix (also a GAN model), are among the top-performing approaches in restoration. Given the difficulty of obtain-ing ground truth images for training palimpsest restoration models, all the aforementioned deep learning methods generated synthetic data using various techniques. Meanwhile, despite the gen-eral consensus that diffusion models may achieve better results than GANs in other fields (Dhariwal and Nichol, 2021; Müller-Franzes et al., 2023), there have been relatively few investigations of their application in palimpsest restoration. Latent diffusion models were investigated by Jampour (2025), who also introduced the Synthetic Geor-gian Palimpsest datasets (Jampour et al., 2024). However, their evaluation was limited to only a few palimpsests, leaving it unclear how well the method generalises across diverse datasets. Palimpsest (under-text) recognition remains an under-explored area, with the earliest studies emerging as late as 2017 (Starynska et al., 2017), focusing primarily on character-level Op-tical Character Recognition (OCR). Subsequent works have also employed OCR/HTR accuracy as an indirect measure of restoration quality (Staryn-ska et al., 2021; Madi et al., 2023). To the best of our knowledge, no known studies have attempted word-level under-text HTR that generalises across diverse manuscripts, and existing approaches re-main confined to specific manuscript types. Because annotated palimpsest data are scarce, current studies rely on synthetic datasets that closely imitate real manuscripts. This approach enables supervised training and quantitative evalu-ation of text separation models. Synthetic genera-tion also allows controlled creation of target masks for text separation and labels for recognition. With the emergence of the CLIP model in vision-language tasks (Radford et al., 2021), its potential for vision-language alignment (Petryk et al., 2022) has yet to receive substantial attention from researchers working on HTR and palimpsest recognition. Existing studies in Scene Text Recog-nition have demonstrated the latent OCR capabil-ities of CLIP, for example through the incorpora-tion of CLIP-based cross-modal alignment plug-ins with existing detection heads (Yu et al., 2023) and the integration of CLIP encoders with multi-branch decoders (Zhao et al., 2024a). Other re-search exploring CLIP in OCR tasks has investi-gated innovative alignment and distillation tech-niques (Wang et al., 2023) as well as integration with large language models (Zhao et al., 2024b). In this work, CLIP is employed as a guidance model whose text-conditioned activation maps, obtained via gradient-weighted attention rollout (Chefer et al., 2021), are used to supervise the Grad-CAM (Selvaraju et al., 2017) attention of the HTR model (in our case the attention-based HTR model (Kass and Vats, 2022)), aiming to enhance modality alignment. This work also evaluates the Pix2pix GAN model, and designs a custom diffu-sion model for under-text retrieval and restoration tasks. Model generalisation and reproducibility are of critical importance; therefore, we conduct a comprehensive evaluation across three datasets: Synthetic MNIST, Synthetic Saint Gall, and Syn-thetic Georgian Palimpsest. One of the few well-known real palimpsest datasets is the Archimedes dataset (Toth and Emery, 2008). However, while some under-text is visible, it lacks paired samples of overlapping and clean under-text, making it un-suitable for training our generative models. It is important to note that no sample selection is performed in this work and the evaluations are conducted across the predefined data splits in ac-cordance with the dataset guidelines and evalua-tion metrics. We believe this will greatly help fa-cilitate future research and benchmarking. 

3 Methodology 

Our primary objective is to recognise the under-text from a synthetic palimpsest. This can be ap-proached in two ways: directly perform HTR on the partially visible under-text, or first carry out text layer separation to reconstruct the under-text image, then perform HTR on the reconstructed im-age. Experiments are conducted on both methods, with the results presented later in section 4.3. In this section we first introduce our text separation techniques, followed by the HTR methods. A palimpsest image x can be viewed approxi-mately as a superposition of the over-text xover , the under-text xunder and the background writing sup-port xbg . The text separation stage (section 3.1) in our pipeline aims to leverage generative mod-els to extract signals from the under-text as an im-age, and the recognition stage (section 3.2) uses an HTR model to identify the text from such images with the guidance of the CLIP model. The overall architecture of the integrated pipeline is shown in Figure 1. The following subsections describe the text separation and HTR models. 

3.1 Palimpsest Text Separation 

The text separation process can be expressed as training a model that takes the palimpsest image x

as input, and reconstructs the under-text xunder . In the subsequent section we will denote the ground truth under-text xunder as the training target y, and introduce the two text separation models imple-mented in this work. 

3.1.1 Pix2pix 

Pix2pix is a conditional GAN with skip connec-tions, demonstrating state-of-the-art performance in text separation for palimpsests on synthetic data (Madi et al., 2023). The architecture is well-suited for image-to-image translation, enabling the sep-aration of under-text, over-text, and background. Traditional methods often fail in overlapping re-gions (Starynska, 2023), whereas Pix2pix pre-serves structural integrity through its U-Net gen-erator and adversarial training, yielding higher-quality under-text reconstruction. The adversar-ial loss further enforces realism in the generated outputs (Isola et al., 2017), allowing conditional GANs to model inter-pixel dependencies absent in standard GANs. Conditional GANs such as Pix2pix learn to map an input image x to a target image ˆy through two competing networks: a generator G and a discrim-inator D. G aims to produce ˆy indistinguishable from the target real image y, while D learns to differentiate between them. Their adversarial in-terplay leads both networks toward optimality. Pix2pix employs a U-Net generator, an encoder-decoder architecture that first downsamples the in-put feature x and then reconstructs through up-sampling. 3.1.2 cDDM 

Historical palimpsests are highly diverse and con-tain degraded script where the unwanted com-ponents (e.g., over-text and complex background noises) are highly structured and do not follow a Gaussian distribution. Therefore, standard De-noising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPM) (Ho et al., 2020), which rely on stochastic Gaus-sian noise mapping, may discard relevant informa-tion when applied to palimpsests. In this work, we propose a custom conditional Denoising Diffusion Model (cDDM). Instead of using randomly sam-pled noise, cDDM adapts the diffusion architec-ture to invert a fixed, structured residual and op-erates as a deterministic diffusion process. This framing aligns with the generalised diffusion liter-ature (e.g., Cold Diffusion (Bansal et al., 2023)), which demonstrates that the diffusion framework can successfully invert arbitrary, non-probabilistic image degradations without relying on Gaussian assumptions. 3.2 Language-Guided Text Recognition 

The palimpsest HTR learning problem can be for-mulated as follows. Given a palimpsest dataset for an HTR task T , let each image contain some re-gion of pixels that is irrelevant to the task (e.g. over-text, damages, degradations) and a region of pixels that is relevant (e.g. under-text). The goal is to recognise the under-text from certain regions in the under-text image (either the raw image or the reconstructed image from the text separation). In this work, we propose language-guided HTR that leverages a vision-language model to guide a conventional HTR system by constraining it to focus on a more semantically grounded attention map. The architecture is shown in the right half of Figure 1, starting from the under-text image that has been recovered from a given input palimpsest image using the text separation model discussed in the previous section (Pix2pix or cDDM). For every image y (or retrieved image ˆy) and ground truth text label ylabel , we create a natural language guiding text τ that captures the key se-mantic concept relevant to T . This guiding text is derived from the dataset’s ground truth label. To ensure compatibility with a pretrained vision-language model like CLIP, τ consists of descrip-tive phrases formatted similarly to CLIP’s default notation. For example, we prepend a structured prompt such as “A photo of a handwritten word 

label ”, where the word label is extracted from the dataset’s ground truth text label ylabel .For an HTR model fθ with parameters θ, we aim to learn an optimal HTR model that outputs a text prediction and an activation map for a given under-text image as input. To learn an optimal fθ,we can estimate a probability map over the latent space, where each entry corresponds to the prob-ability that pixel is relevant to τ . In this work, we build on the AttentionHTR model (Kass and Vats, 2022) (AHTR in short). In principle, any HTR model that can generate intermediate acti-vation maps and support a standard classification loss could be used. Next, we introduce language-guided supervi-sion by generating ground truth activation maps from CLIP. We refer to the resulting model as G-AHTR (Guided-AttentionHTR). CLIP is a vision-language model that takes both an image and a text input and produces a similarity score in a shared embedding space. Although this score is global, gradient-based attribution allows us to propagate it through CLIP’s visual encoder to obtain pixel-level relevance maps (Petryk et al., 2022), enabling us to exploit CLIP’s text-image alignment without modifying the base HTR archi-tecture. Since our CLIP visual encoder is a ViT-B/32 transformer, we compute these maps with gradient-weighted attention rollout (Chefer et al., 2021), accumulating the gradient-weighted atten-tion of all transformer blocks into a relevancy map over the visual tokens. Before extracting activation maps, we fine-tune the full CLIP ViT-B/32 model on the training split of the respective (reconstructed) dataset with the standard symmetric image–text contrastive loss (temperature 0.07 ), using AdamW with learning rate 10 −6 and weight decay 10 −4 for 20 epochs; the HTR-sized grayscale images are replicated to three channels and bilinearly resized to 224 × 224 .Given the under-text image y and guiding text 

τ , we feed both into CLIP (input resized to 224 ×

224 ) and obtain a text-conditioned relevance map over the visual tokens, which for the ViT-B/32 en-coder natively lies on a 7 × 7 grid. This map is bilinearly upsampled to the image resolution, un-dergoes the same crop-and-pad preprocessing as the input image (Section 4.1), and is bilinearly re-sampled to the HTR input grid, yielding 

ACLIP (y, τ ) : {1, . . . , 32 }×{ 1, . . . , 100 } → [0 , 1] .

Im-portantly, we do not use CLIP’s prediction itself, only its activation map to provide attention guid-ance. 4 Experimental Results 

The proposed methods are empirically evaluated on three synthetic datasets using standard evalua-tion metrics. This section is organised as follows. We begin by presenting the synthetic datasets and detailing the process to create them. Next, we de-scribe the evaluation metrics used. Finally, we present the results for both text separation and recognition across three scenarios: (a) recognition from the original datasets (MNIST and Saint Gall) without overlapping text, serving as a baseline; (b) direct recognition of the partially visible under-text from palimpsest images; and (c) recognition following text layer separation, where the under-text is first reconstructed.

4.1.2 Synthetic Saint Gall 

The Saint Gall dataset (Fischer et al., 2011) orig-inates from a 9th century Latin manuscript and comprises 60 pages of Carolingian minuscule script by a single scribe. It contains 1 410 text lines and 11 597 words. Images were binarised, normalised, and paired with sentence-level labels. Its historical significance, complex script, and ink-on-parchment characteristics make it a valuable benchmark for HTR models. A synthetic version was generated to introduce real historical text and extend processing to larger images with longer words. Under-text was ex-tracted at the word level using provided bound-ing boxes, separated from the background with a threshold of 100, and placed on a transparent 350 ×350 canvas. Words exceeding 350 pixels in width were skipped. Under-text colour and rota-tion followed Eq. 14 and Eq. 13. Backgrounds were generated similarly to the synthetic MNIST dataset with 350 ×350 pixel can-vases, using Eq. 16 for color, uniform noise n ∼

Uniform (1 , 50) (Eq. 17), salt-and-pepper noise ∼

Uniform (0 , 0.04) , and Gaussian noise with 50% probability, σ ∼ Uniform (1 , 3) . Under-text was centred and images resized to 224 ×224 to reduce computation while preserving quality. Over-texts consisted of random Latin strings with random fonts, size 30-40, overlaid on under-text. Prior to recognition, all images are dynamically cropped to ensure a text-focused region and pre-vent geometric distortion. Since our HTR model has a fixed 100 × 32 input resolution, directly re-sizing the original 224 ×224 images severely com-pressed the text due to excess vertical white space. Recognition with text separation: Further-more, we integrate the text separation stage into the HTR pipeline, where Pix2pix and cDDMs are used to reconstruct the under-text from the palimpsest images. The models are trained on the respective training splits of each dataset, with hy-perparameters (such as learning rate, epochs, and cDDM denoising steps) optimised to obtain the best models. The saved model checkpoints are then used to perform reconstructions on the test sets. The separation results are evaluated using the three metrics introduced in Section 4.2: SSIM, PSNR, and FID, as reported in Table 2. Overall, cDDM2 achieves the best performance on Synthetic MNIST and Synthetic Saint Gall, obtaining the highest SSIM on both datasets, to-gether with the lowest FID and near-best PSNR on Synthetic MNIST and the highest PSNR on Synthetic Saint Gall (where Pix2pix retains the lowest FID), demonstrating the effectiveness of our proposed custom noise mechanism. Its per-formance on Synthetic Georgian is slightly lower than that of Pix2pix, primarily due to colour dis-crepancies. This can be observed in the examples in Figure 3, where the cDDM reconstructions ap-pear lighter than those of Pix2pix; however, the under-text shapes are clearly revealed and do not hinder recognition. The MNIST results (first row of Figure 3) further reveal an interesting difference: Pix2pix often behaves more rigidly than cDDM by at-tempting to forcefully recover all visible details, such as the black dot above the true under-text “9”, originating from the over-text “T”. Since AHTR is designed for Latin characters only, its results are not compared with previous works involving other scripts, such as Greek, Syriac, and Hebrew (Madi et al., 2023). As a baseline observation, integrating under-text separation substantially improves recognition ac-curacy over direct HTR on raw palimpsests, par-ticularly on the highly challenging Saint Gall dataset. Among the evaluated pipelines, our pro-posed cDDM2 + G-AHTR framework achieves the best overall performance, yielding the high-est mean WAcc and lowest mean WER on both the Synthetic MNIST and Synthetic Saint Gall datasets, although the margins over cDDM2 + AHTR lie within one standard deviation of the ten runs. In particular, when paired with cDDM2 reconstructions, G-AHTR yields small improve-ments in word-level metrics (WAcc/WER), while CER does not improve (1.98 vs. 1.95 on Synthetic Saint Gall); with Overlaid and Pix2pix inputs, G-AHTR performs on par with or slightly below AHTR, suggesting that the guidance is most useful when the reconstructed under-text is of high qual-ity. This disparity is expected, as the high vari-ability and ambiguity inherent in handwritten text make character-level recognition more challeng-ing. It is worth noticing that the language guiding mechanism comes at no cost on training stability, since the standard deviations of G-AHTR results are around the same level as those of AHTR. While the absolute performance gains provided by the language-guided supervision are modest, we hypothesise that the performance ceiling is constrained by the following primary factors: i) the inherent limitations of CLIP in fine-grained handwriting recognition, which become more ob-vious when the intermediate recovered under-text remains highly degraded; ii) gradient competition between the primary CE loss and the auxiliary ac-tivation constraint, as strict spatial alignment with CLIP’s feature maps can be partially orthogonal to the optimal feature representations needed for pure character decoding. 5 Conclusion 

This work advances automated palimpsest re-covery by integrating structured noise modelling, generative reconstruction, and language-grounded recognition. We design a custom denoising dif-fusion model to capture layered degradation, ex-plore Pix2pix GANs for restoration, and incor-porate textual priors from vision-language atten-tion maps for under-text recognition. Experi-ments demonstrate improved performance across diverse benchmarks. In particular, the cDDM2 + G-AHTR pipeline achieves the best word-level re-sults and competitive overall scores, while yield-ing more interpretable attention behaviour.
